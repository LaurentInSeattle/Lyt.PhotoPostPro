# Camera Connection 

Click the camera icon on the navigation bar on the left side to enter 'Camera' mode.

Connect your camera or iPhone via USB to your personal computer. 
Thousands of camera model are supported. All MTP compatible devices will connect to Photo Rebel.
Do not forget to turn your camera on or 'wake' it up, if needed.

The User Interface should then display the name of the connected camera and the 'Begin Transfer' button should light up. 
Launch the transfer and watch your photo being downloaded from your device, showing up as thumbnails in the main view. 
Depending on how many images are stored on your camera, this may take a while. 
Once all images have been downloaded or when you interrupt the transfer, you have the oportunity to bring them all in your library 
or select some to ignore, or just pick a few to include in your library.

Once you are done with this selection process, you can optionally delete some or all images in the camera. 
Important Note: Apple iPhone will NOT let Photo Rebel (or any other program) delete images or other files 
from your device.

If movie files or other files have been doanloaded, you can switch to the corresponding view and 
repeat the same selection and delete process.

# Your Library 

Click the 'stack of photos' icon on the navigation bar on the left side to enter 'Library' mode.

Images added to your library, either imported or downloaded from your camera are classified by Year, Month and Day.
There are three dates of importance for Photo Rebel: 
- Date of Capture : When the photo was shot.
- Date of Addition in your Library : When the photo was actually in your library.
- Date of Last Edit : When you edited the photo for the last time.

The library view top area let you select any combination of date kind with any choice of Year, Month and Day.
The library view then filters and shows the corresponding images.

# -B- The Tag System 

Tagging your photos with keywords, locations, and names is important because it turns a massive, 
unorganized digital pile into a searchable library.

You will:
- Find images quickly: Typing a word like "dog" or a specific place name lets you locate one photo out of thousands in seconds.
- Save time: You avoid scrolling endlessly through years of random pictures to find a single memory.

Benefits:
- Tags let you sort images by people, places, events, or objects.
- Years later, a location or name tag reminds you exactly where a picture was taken or who was in it.

Tagging is optional with Photo Rebel... But is strongly recommended.

Each time an image is selected in the library, a larger thumbnail and its metadata is shown in the right panel, 
along with a 'Tags' section.

# The Star System 

Rating photos with star systems or labels is very important in photo editing because it turns a potentially overwhelming 
mass of raw images into an organized, efficient workflow.

With Photo Rebel the Star System is conceptually very simple: 

- Zero Star: this photo has never been reviewed yet.

- One Star: Poor image, keep for now, but consider delete.
- Two Stars: Not so good.
- Three Stars: Not bad at all.
- Four Stars: A keeper.
- Five Stars: Gallery worthy photo, your best!


This rating system is very similar to what is available in almost all other photo editors. 
Photo Rebel adds the following tweaks: 

- Zero Star: This default rating is used when launching a culling process.
- Five Stars: Those images are aautomatically added to your 'Gallery'. (See corresponding section) 

Here are a few key reasons why ratings matter: 

Speeds Up Culling: Sorting through hundreds or thousands of shots becomes fast when you browse your library with filters.

Reduces Decision Fatigue: Separating keepers from discards in multiple quick passes stops you from 
over-analyzing every single file.

Creates Editing Priorities: You can filter your software library to show only high-priority photos, 
ensuring you edit your best work first.

Simplifies Portfolio Building: Consistent ratings create a permanent, searchable record of 
your top-tier images for sharing, printing, or publishing.

Ideally, all images in your library should be at least 'Two Stars'.

Each time an image is selected in the library, a larger thumbnail and its metadata is shown in the right panel, 
along with the 'Tags' section. Clicking the stars will let you change the rating given to that particular picture.

# Culling 

Culling is the very important process of reviewing a large batch of raw photos and selecting only the strongest 
images while discarding duplicates, blurry shots, and technical mistakes before you start editing.

-B- Why Culling is Important? 

It saves time: Editing and tagging every single photo from a photoshoot wastes hours. 
Culling cuts down the number of files so you only spend time on the best shots.
It improves quality: Removing duplicate angles and minor blunders ensures that every image you present 
meets a high quality standard.
It prevents fatigue: Looking at hundreds of near-identical pictures might wears out your creative energy. 
Culling early keeps your mind fresh for actual editing choices.
It saves storage space: High-resolution digital files take up massive amounts of hard drive and cloud capacity. Deleting unused raw files frees up valuable storage.
It is often more satisfying: We often prefer a tight, curated gallery of amazing moments over a bloated collection filled 
with repetitive frames.

All professional photographers have told me many times that I should cull more aggressively, 
now I do so and maybe you do so as well.

# Culling common mistakes

Editing before culling. Fix the exposure, move on, hit a better version of the same shot ten images later. 
The time spent on the first frame is wasted. Cull the entire set before opening your editing software.

Keeping images out of emotional attachment. The first time you photograph something — a new venue, a sport 
you're unfamiliar with, a type of subject — you'll want to keep images because of what they represent to you. 
Assess the image, not the experience of taking it.

Delivering too many similar images. Giving a client 12 nearly identical frames of the same moment doesn't 
show abundance — it shows you didn't edit with any rigour. Select the one.

Over-trusting automated AI on emotionally complex material. 
Full automation works well for technically consistent shooting. 
If your work has variance, build in a review pass after automation — especially for moments that might appear 
technically compromised but carry the story.

# Why Do My Edited Photos Look Bad?

If your edits look “off” even though you’re trying all the sliders, you’re not alone. 
Most edited photos look bad for a few predictable reasons: too much contrast, weird color, crunchy sharpness, 
or edits that fight the original light.

This section breaks down the most common mistakes (and quick fixes) so your photos look clean, natural, and intentionally edited—without the overprocessed vibe.

1) The Edit Is Too Strong (The #1 Problem)

When an edit looks bad, it’s usually not the idea—it’s the intensity. Phones and social apps make it easy to push sliders too far.

    Fix: Reduce your adjustments by 30–50% and compare.
    Quick check: Toggle “before/after.” If the edit shocks you, it’s too much.

2) Highlights Are Blown Out (Or Shadows Are Crushed)

Bright skies, windows, and white clothing get ruined fast. On the other side, deep shadows can turn into muddy black patches with no detail.

    Fix: Lower highlights first, then adjust exposure.
    Fix: Lift shadows slightly, then add a touch of contrast back.
    Rule: Keep detail in the brightest and darkest areas when possible.

3) White Balance Is Off (Everything Looks “Weird”)

Bad color is the fastest way to make an edit look amateur. If skin looks orange, shadows look green, or the photo feels cold and lifeless—white balance is the culprit.

    Fix: Adjust temperature until whites look neutral (not yellow/blue).
    Fix: Use tint to correct green/magenta casts (common indoors).
    Tip: Don’t “guess.” Zoom in on something that should be neutral (a white wall, gray shirt, paper).

4) Too Much Clarity/Structure/Texture (The “Crunchy” Look)

Clarity and texture can make landscapes and buildings pop, but they can wreck portraits and make everything look harsh, gritty, and over-sharpened.

    Fix: Pull back clarity/structure/texture until skin and edges look natural.
    Portrait rule: If pores, wrinkles, or noise suddenly dominate the photo, you went too far.

5) Over-Sharpening (Especially on Phone Photos)

Phone images are already sharpened by default. Adding more sharpness often creates halos around edges and makes noise look worse.

    Fix: Reduce sharpening, then add a little noise reduction if needed.
    Zoom rule: Check at 100% zoom before exporting.

6) Saturation Is Too High (Cartoon Colors)

Heavy saturation makes blues neon, skin orange, and greens radioactive. It also destroys subtle tones and looks fake fast.

    Fix: Prefer vibrance over saturation for a more natural boost.
    Fix: If skin looks sunburnt, saturation is almost always the reason.

7) You’re Editing a Photo That Was Badly Lit

Editing can enhance a good photo—but it can’t fully rescue harsh overhead sun, mixed indoor lighting, or super dark blurry shots. If the light is fighting you, your edit will look worse the harder you push it.

To improve your edits long-term, focus on better light first:

8) Filters + Manual Edits Are Stacking (And It Gets Messy)

Many people apply a filter, then also crank exposure/contrast/saturation on top. That stacking is a common reason edits look muddy or unnatural.

    Fix: Start clean (no filter), then edit. Or use a light filter and do minimal slider work.
    Fix: If using presets, treat them like a starting point—not the final look.


Quick Answer: What Makes an Edit Look “Professional”?

    Balanced exposure (highlights not blown, shadows not muddy)
    Natural color (especially skin tones)
    Intentional contrast (depth without harshness)
    Consistency across a set (same vibe, same color logic)
